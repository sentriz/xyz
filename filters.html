1 · The reconstruction filter's runway

A DAC's reconstruction filter must stay flat to 20 kHz (the audible band) and be gone by Nyquist: playback creates mirror images of the spectrum above Nyquist that must be removed before they stress amplifiers and tweeters. (On the recording side the ADC's anti-alias filter has the same runway, there to stop ultrasonics folding down into the audio.) 44.1 kHz gives the filter 2.05 kHz to get there; 48 kHz gives it 4 kHz.

The ratio between the two rates is unchanged.)

2 · FIR and IIR — what the names mean

Feed a filter a single one-sample click (an impulse) and watch what comes out. That output — the impulse response — is the filter's complete fingerprint, and its shape is what the names describe.

FIR — Finite Impulse Response

No feedback: the output is a weighted sum of the last N inputs. Each weight is a tap. After N samples the click has fully passed through — the response ends. Symmetric taps ⇒ every frequency is delayed equally (linear phase).

IIR — Infinite Impulse Response

Feedback: the output is fed back into itself, like a plucked string. The ring decays but never exactly reaches zero — hence infinite. A handful of coefficients buys a lot of filtering, but frequencies near the cutoff exit later than the rest.

The FIR above is 47 taps of a windowed sinc — the textbook lowpass. The IIR is a single biquad (2 feedback + 3 feedforward coefficients), the building block almost all audio EQs, crossovers, and tone controls are made of. Five numbers versus forty-seven: that economy is why IIR exists.

Is this digital-only? The names come from DSP, but the split maps onto physics. Every practical analog filter — RC networks, LC speaker crossovers, the Butterworth below — is IIR by nature: capacitors and inductors store energy and release it exponentially, so the response decays forever. A true FIR needs delayed copies of the signal, and delay is trivial digitally (a sample buffer) but exotic in analog. It does exist — SAW filters in RF front-ends tap an acoustic wave with metal fingers along a crystal, a genuinely analog FIR — but for audio, FIR only became practical when the signal became numbers.

3 · Same cutoff, different behavior

Both filters below are lowpasses aimed at the 48 kHz runway: pass 20 kHz, kill 24 kHz. The FIR is 45 taps (Kaiser design; 23 multiplies once you fold the symmetric halves); the IIR is an 8th-order Butterworth, drawn as its classic analog prototype curve. Watch both charts, not just the first.

Magnitude — how much gets through

In a 4 kHz transition band the FIR reaches ≈−60 dB; the analog Butterworth's smooth slope has barely started falling. Steep-and-narrow is FIR territory.

Group delay — when it gets through

The FIR delays every frequency identically: a flat line, pure time-shift. The IIR delays frequencies near its cutoff the most — transients get smeared. This is what "linear phase" buys.

A caveat on the curves: the Butterworth plotted is the analog prototype — what an analog implementation, or a digital one running far above audio rates, would do. An actual digital Butterworth sampled at 48 kHz with its cutoff this close to Nyquist behaves differently: the bilinear transform's frequency warping squeezes its slope much steeper (and grows its delay bump to match). The phase lesson is unchanged; the exact magnitude numbers belong to the analog case.

So why does your DAC use FIR? The anti-alias job is exactly the shape IIR is bad at: a brutally narrow transition band and a need for flat phase across the whole audible range. The tap cost is tamed by structure rather than avoided: the delta-sigma converter oversamples internally (64–512×), and the one genuinely sharp filter runs once, at the lowest rate, as a half-band FIR — symmetry forces half its taps to be exactly zero. Every stage after it, including the final analog smoothing filter, sees a runway of hundreds of kHz where filtering is nearly free.

4 · Phase — when each frequency arrives

Magnitude says how much of each frequency survives; phase says when it comes out. Filters with identical magnitude can time-shift frequencies completely differently. Below, the section-3 FIR is rebuilt two ways: the magnitudes match to 0.01 dB, but the impulse responses have opposite personalities.

Linear phase

Symmetric around the peak at tap 22 — so it rings before the transient as well as after (pre-ringing). Every frequency is delayed by exactly the same 22 samples.

Minimum phase

Same magnitude, energy packed as early as mathematically possible: the peak is at tap 0 and all ringing trails after the transient — the way familiar analog filters behave.

Magnitude — one visible curve, because they're identical

The wide red band is the minimum-phase response drawn underneath the blue linear-phase one. Magnitude alone cannot tell these filters apart.

Group delay — where they differ

Linear phase: every frequency waits the same 0.46 ms. Minimum phase: almost everything exits nearly immediately, with a small wait near the cutoff — the least delay any filter with this magnitude can have.

Why anyone cares: a minimum-phase filter's ringing hides behind the transient, where the ear masks it; linear phase rings before the transient too (pre-echo), which nothing in nature does. That argument carries real weight for filters acting inside the audible band (EQ, lossy codecs); for a reconstruction filter ringing at ~22 kHz its audibility is contested at best — yet it is exactly what the "filter: sharp / slow / minimum phase" menu on hi-fi DACs is choosing between. In exchange, linear phase keeps the waveform's shape perfectly intact across the band — no frequency smearing — which is why DAC reconstruction filters default to it and mastering EQs offer it as a mode, with its fixed latency as the price. Two useful facts fall out of "minimum": its phase is fully determined by its magnitude (there is no freedom left), and it is the only version whose inverse is also stable (provided the magnitude never dips to exactly zero) — which is why room-correction systems invert only the minimum-phase part of what they measure.
